# Turkey & Greece – 11 Day Travel Blog (7/11/25 - 7/21/25)

This travelogue captures an unforgettable 11-day journey across Turkey and Greece,
combining iconic landmarks, cultural discoveries, and personal reflections.
The trip was fast-paced yet deeply enriching, with moments of awe, learning, and light-hearted anecdotes.
Each day blends historical context with first-hand experiences to help future travelers plan their own adventures.

---

## Day 0 – The Journey Begins

**Route:** San Jose → Dallas → London → Istanbul

Our trip began before the sun even thought about rising. At **3:00 AM**,
we left home for San Jose Airport, starting our first international vacation as U.S. citizens.
The itinerary had been altered months earlier when British Airways canceled one of our flights,
forcing us into a more complex route via Dallas and London.

The Dallas-to-London leg brought a small frustration — our initial seat allocation had each of us
scattered across different rows. After some effort, we managed to sit closer together, although still
not ideal. The tightest moment came during our London Heathrow transfer — just **1 hour 20 minutes**
between flights. Every step felt like a race against time, but we made it.

By the time we landed in Istanbul’s **European-side airport**, we were exhausted but excited.
Our transfer driver, **Fatih**, arrived about 20 minutes late, giving me my first taste of the
Turkish tendency to keep visitors waiting. Along the way, I learned quirky local facts: Istanbul is called the
*“City of Seven Hills”*, speeding fines can reach **5,000 TRY**, and exiting your vehicle
during an accident can cost **180,000 TRY** in fines.

The hour-long drive to **Arts Taksim Hotel** gave us our first look at the sprawling,
traffic-heavy city.

## Day 1 – Istanbul & Taksim Square

**Morning to Afternoon:** Arrival and check-in at **Arts Taksim Hotel**, centrally located near **Taksim Square** — just a stone’s throw away.

We began our Turkish journey with a short walk along the historic tram line. The square was buzzing — a blend of tourists, locals, and vendors, with **Taksim Mosque** and the **Republic Monument** adding historic depth to the area.

We grabbed a bite — shawarma-style pita rolls (though not called “shawarma” here), costing **380 TRY** — and later visited a cafeteria-style restaurant where you choose your dishes before paying. The *vegetable-stuffed rice* was a standout, and dinner cost **1,050 TRY**.

Although I had hoped to visit **Istiklal Street** for some light shopping and maybe a Turkish ice cream, fatigue caught up with us. At the far end of our walk, I wondered about a monument we didn’t quite reach — something to explore next time.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* Taksim Square isn’t just a tourist hub — it has been the stage for numerous political gatherings and protests in modern Turkish history.
* The historic red tram still runs, a nostalgic reminder of Istanbul’s past.
* I quickly noticed the expectation for tips in almost every transaction — something travelers should budget for.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Stay near Taksim Square if you want walkable access to shops, restaurants, and public transport.
2. Expect crowds and street hustle — it’s busy day and night.
3. Don’t skip the tram ride for a quick and charming tour of the main street.
4. If arriving after a long international journey, keep Day 1 light to adjust to the time zone.

## Day 2 – Istanbul City Tour: Blue Mosque, Hagia Sophia, Topkapi Palace, Bosphorus Cruise, Grand Bazaar

**Morning:** We kicked off our full-day guided tour with some of Istanbul’s most iconic landmarks.

### Blue Mosque (Sultan Ahmed Mosque)

Built between 1609–1616 under Sultan Ahmed I, the Blue Mosque is one of Istanbul’s defining sights.
It is named after the **20,000 İznik tiles** inside, many in shades of blue, which glow beautifully in the morning light.
A detail that struck me was the **iron chain** at the entrance — a symbolic reminder that even the Sultan had to bow his head in humility upon entering.

### Hagia Sophia (Ayasofya)

Few buildings in the world carry such layered history. Originally built in **537 CE** by Emperor Justinian, Hagia Sophia was the world’s largest cathedral for nearly a thousand years.
Later it became a mosque, then a museum, and in 2020, reverted back into a mosque.
While its dome and mosaics were breathtaking, I felt a letdown here: non-Turkish visitors are not allowed on the first floor (the gallery), which limits the experience.
Still, its blend of Christian mosaics and Islamic calligraphy makes it unique in the world.

### Topkapi Palace

From the 15th to the mid-19th century, Topkapi was the administrative heart of the Ottoman Empire and the residence of sultans.
We explored courtyards, kiosks, and chambers filled with artifacts.
A highlight was the **Spoonmaker’s Diamond**, an 86-carat pear-shaped jewel — one of the largest in the world.
Wandering these halls, I couldn’t help but imagine the intrigues and power struggles that once played out here.

### The Hippodrome & Egyptian Obelisk

Our guide shared an anecdote about the obelisk brought from Egypt. Originally erected in Karnak, it was transported to Constantinople but broke during the journey.
For centuries, it lay in Alexandria, waiting for the technology and ships to catch up before it was finally re-erected in the Hippodrome.

### Grand Bazaar

One of the world’s oldest covered markets, dating back to the 15th century, the **Grand Bazaar** is a labyrinth of 4,000+ shops.
We shopped for saffron and gifts but quickly learned the importance of comparison — I paid **$17/gram** for saffron in one place,
only to find another shop selling it for **$5/gram**.
Still, the experience of bargaining and exploring was a thrill.

### Bosphorus Cruise (Evening)

In the evening, we joined a **3-hour Bosphorus cruise**. The length felt unnecessarily long, but the sights made it worthwhile —
the glittering city skyline, the Dolmabahçe Palace along the shore, Ottoman mansions, and the famous Bosphorus and Fatih Sultan Mehmet bridges connecting Europe and Asia.
Dinner featured fresh snapper fish — a standout meal in Istanbul.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* Hagia Sophia once stored gunpowder in its dome, which caused catastrophic damage when hit during an attack.
* The Blue Mosque’s six minarets caused controversy — it matched the number at Mecca, so a seventh was added there to settle the dispute.
* Smoking is strikingly common in Turkey — I noticed even women in full hijab openly smoking, something that felt unusual compared to what I’m used to.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Dress modestly when visiting mosques; carry a scarf for Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque.
2. Allow at least half a day for Topkapi Palace — it’s not just one building but a complex of courtyards and pavilions.
3. At the Grand Bazaar, never buy from the first shop you see; prices vary wildly.
4. The Bosphorus Cruise is worth it but consider a shorter 1.5-hour option for a more comfortable experience.
5. If planning an early morning flight (like ours to Cappadocia the next day), keep the evening light — we only managed 4 hours of sleep before our next adventure.

## Day 3 – Cappadocia: Valleys, Fairy Chimneys, Pottery & Cave Hotel

### Early Morning

We left Istanbul on an early flight to Cappadocia, departing from the Asian-side airport.
The check-in process was automated — printing bag tags, weighing luggage, and sending them off ourselves —
but the sheer crowds and glitches made it a stressful experience.
To add to this, our guide from Memphis Tours was more focused on leaving early than actually helping us,
making the morning tougher than expected.

After landing at **Kayseri Airport**, we drove 1.5 hours into Cappadocia’s dreamlike terrain.

### Paşabağ (Monks Valley)

Our first stop was Paşabağ, with its mushroom-shaped fairy chimneys — natural formations carved by volcanic activity and erosion.
Early Christian monks once lived in these chimneys, carving chapels and hermit dwellings.
The valley, part of Cappadocia’s UNESCO heritage designation, felt timeless.

### Valley of Imagination (Devrent Valley)

Unlike other valleys, Devrent is purely nature’s canvas. Here, erosion has shaped rocks into animals and whimsical figures.
The famous “Camel Rock” stood out, but part of the charm was simply letting your imagination fill in the rest.

### Avanos Pottery Village (Kybele Pottery Center)

Avanos, by the Kızılırmak River, has been producing pottery since Hittite times.
At Kybele Pottery Center, we saw artisans molding red clay into intricate shapes.
My wife even tried her hand at the wheel. I couldn’t resist buying a **$250 vase** — an impulsive decision that later felt too influenced,
but also added a story to our travels.

### Ortahisar

This “Middle Castle” has been a lookout since Byzantine times. From its rocky heights,
it offered sweeping views of valleys and Mount Erciyes in the distance.
Its interior, with storage rooms and tunnels, showed how the locals ingeniously adapted their landscape.

### Dovecote Valley (Pigeon Valley)

We hiked through cliffs filled with pigeon houses carved into rock. These weren’t decorative;
locals relied on pigeon droppings as fertilizer for their vineyards.
It was a long, somewhat strenuous walk, but also a lesson in how people once sustained themselves with simple, natural systems.

### Uçhisar Castle

At Cappadocia’s highest vantage point, Uçhisar Castle rises like a natural skyscraper.
Its honeycombed rooms and tunnels made me wonder how entire communities lived here centuries ago.
From the top, we had panoramic views of Cappadocia’s valleys, a fitting finale for the day’s sightseeing.

### Carpet Workshop

Our day ended at a carpet-weaving center. Turkish carpets are known worldwide, often taking months or years to weave by hand.
What once was a dowry tradition has become a global art form.
Despite promising myself restraint, I left with a **4x6 carpet for $400** — another impulsive buy I justified as “cultural appreciation.”

### Helike Cave Hotel (Ürgüp)

Our cave hotel was a unique experience. Staying in a room carved into rock felt authentic and atmospheric, though not entirely comfortable.
Still, it was something I wouldn’t have missed.

### ⚠ The Big Miss: Underground City

It was only later that I realized a glaring omission: we weren’t taken to Cappadocia’s **underground city** (like Özkonak or Derinkuyu).
These multi-level subterranean cities, once shelters for early Christians against invaders, are among Cappadocia’s most fascinating sites.
Looking back, skipping this was a real disappointment, and I wish our guide had included it.
For future travelers, make sure this is part of your itinerary.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* Cappadocia’s fairy chimneys are the result of millions of years of volcanic activity and erosion.
* Pigeon houses here were critical to agriculture — pigeon manure fertilized vineyards.
* Both pottery and carpet-making traditions go back thousands of years and remain central to Cappadocia’s culture.
* Missing the underground city meant missing one of Cappadocia’s most unique attractions — double-check your itinerary.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Confirm in advance that your tour includes an underground city visit — it’s a highlight, not an optional extra.
2. Expect guides of varying quality; be ready to ask questions and keep them engaged.
3. Cappadocia involves lots of walking on uneven ground — bring sturdy shoes and water.
4. Be mindful at pottery and carpet shops; sales pitches can be persuasive. Set a budget before you go.
5. A cave hotel stay is worth it for the experience, even if the comfort isn’t perfect.

## Day 4 – Hot Air Balloon Experience & Transfer to the Aegean Coast

### Early Morning – Hot Air Balloon Ride

This day began before dawn with Cappadocia’s most iconic activity: the **hot air balloon ride**.
We were picked up around 4:00 AM by **Kaya Balloons**, one of the region’s reputable operators.
The preparation itself was spectacular — watching the balloons slowly inflate in the dark, lit by bursts of flame from the burners, felt like a scene from another world.

By sunrise, over **160 balloons** were floating in the skies above Cappadocia, filling the horizon with a surreal tapestry of color.
Our balloon rose gently and eventually reached a height of around **700 meters (2,300 feet)**,
high enough to take in sweeping views of the fairy chimneys, valleys, vineyards, and volcanic ridges.

The stillness of the flight, interrupted only by the occasional roar of the burners, made it a once-in-a-lifetime experience.
Looking down on Cappadocia’s lunar-like landscape from such heights was breathtaking.
It’s easy to see why Cappadocia is called the *“hot air balloon capital of the world”* — nowhere else do so many balloons take off at once, creating such a magical atmosphere.

We floated for about an hour before descending gently back to earth, completing an adventure that was worth every bit of the early morning wake-up.

### Midday – Flight to Izmir

After returning to the hotel for breakfast and a quick freshen-up, we checked out and headed to the airport for our midday flight to **Izmir**.
By this point, the heat was punishing, with temperatures around **38°C**, making the cool breeze of the Aegean coast all the more appealing.

The short flight lasted about 80 minutes, and upon arrival in Izmir, we were met by our driver for a **1.5-hour transfer** to our hotel near Kuşadası.
The drive hugged parts of the Aegean coastline, with glimpses of seaside towns along the way.

### Evening – Arrival at the Aegean Coast

We checked in at **Grand Belish Hotel**, a seaside resort in Belgesi, about 20 minutes from Kuşadası.
The property sat directly on the edge of the Aegean Sea, offering stunning ocean views.
After the constant motion of the first few days, it was a welcome chance to slow down and relax.
We finally got some much-needed sleep, recharging for the historical heavyweights waiting the next day in **Ephesus**.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* On the morning of our ride, **160+ balloons** were in the air at once — a spectacle unique to Cappadocia.
* Our balloon reached **700 meters** in height, offering panoramic views across the region.
* Cappadocia operates balloon rides on over **260 days a year**, making it the most reliable ballooning destination in the world.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Book the hot air balloon early — flights depend on weather, so it’s best to schedule it for your first morning in Cappadocia to allow rescheduling if needed.
2. Bring warm layers for the early morning balloon ride; it gets chilly before sunrise even in summer.
3. Flying into Izmir and staying near Kuşadası is ideal for visiting Ephesus the next day, while also enjoying a relaxing seaside stay.
4. Use this day as a transition and rest period — the following days are packed with sightseeing.
5. Don’t skip time to just enjoy the Aegean coast — it’s a great counterbalance to Cappadocia’s arid landscape.

## Day 5 – Ephesus, Basilica of St. John, House of the Virgin Mary & Leather Fashion Show

### Morning – Drive to Ephesus

We left our seaside hotel in Belgesi for a short **30-minute drive** to one of the world’s most legendary ancient sites: **Ephesus**.

Once a thriving port city, Ephesus dates back to around **1000 BC** and remained prominent until the 13th century.
Over time, the sea receded, forcing the city to relocate closer to the new coastline.
Walking its marble streets today, it’s hard to imagine it was once buzzing with traders, philosophers, and religious leaders.

### Ephesus Highlights

* **Temple of Artemis (Diana):** Once one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, only a single reconstructed pillar remains today.
  Though we could only view it from afar, its legacy as a grand temple to the fertility goddess is undeniable.
* **Nike Relief:** A beautifully carved figure of Nike, the goddess of victory, adorned the ruins.
  Her winged figure is said to have inspired the modern sports brand’s iconic “swoosh” logo.
* **Great Theatre:** Designed to hold **25,000 spectators**, this theatre hosted not only performances but also political assemblies.
  Its sheer scale is awe-inspiring.
* **Roman Toilets:** The communal latrines, complete with rows of marble seats, offered a curious reminder that even politics was born in unusual places —
  as people would sit, talk, and debate while attending to their needs.
  I found it both amusing and fascinating that this site became a place of political discussion.
* **Library of Celsus:** One of the most photographed structures, the library facade reflects Roman architectural grandeur.
  It once held **12,000 scrolls** and served as a mausoleum for its founder.

### Basilica of St. John

Next, we visited the **Basilica of St. John**, commissioned by Emperor Justinian in the 6th century.
It’s believed to be the burial site of the **Apostle John**, one of Jesus’ disciples.
Though now largely ruins, the basilica’s scale and history reflect its former importance as a Christian pilgrimage site.

### House of the Virgin Mary

On a serene hilltop stood the **House of the Virgin Mary**, a modest stone chapel believed to be where Mary spent her last years.
Its association comes from visions described by German nun **Anne Catherine Emmerich** in the 19th century.
Though debated by historians, it is revered by both Christians and Muslims.
The setting was peaceful, with prayer walls where visitors leave written wishes.

### Afternoon – Leather Fashion Show

After lunch, we were taken to a local **leather factory** for a personalized fashion show.
Models strutted in high-end leather jackets, said to be exported to luxury brands like Gucci and Prada.
My wife even had the chance to walk the runway, which was a fun surprise!
The prices, even after a steep **55% discount**, started around **$500** —
we resisted the temptation this time, unlike our pottery and carpet splurges earlier in the trip.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* The Library of Celsus’ facade was reconstructed in the 1970s with fragments found on site.
* The House of the Virgin Mary attracts not only Christians but also Muslims, who revere Mary as the mother of a prophet.
* Nike’s swoosh comes directly from the symbolism of Nike, the goddess of victory.
* “Politics” literally came from people chatting in public toilets like those in Ephesus.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Allocate at least **3–4 hours** to properly explore Ephesus; it’s vast and rich in history.
2. Wear comfortable shoes and hats — it gets very hot, and the marble streets can be slippery.
3. Don’t miss the Nike carving and the communal latrines — small but memorable details.
4. The House of the Virgin Mary is serene and worth visiting, even if you’re not religious.
5. Leather factory visits are fun, but don’t feel pressured to buy — prices are high despite discounts.

## Day 6 – Athens City Tour: Acropolis, Acropolis Museum, City Landmarks & Evening in Plaka

### Early Morning – Arrival in Athens

After arriving from Turkey in the early hours, we barely managed a few hours of rest before our **10:00 AM Athens city tour**.
Running on fumes but powered by excitement, we set off to see the cradle of Western civilization.

### Panathenaic Stadium (Kallimarmaro)

Our first stop was the **Panathenaic Stadium**, built entirely of white marble.
Reconstructed in the 19th century, it hosted the **first modern Olympic Games in 1896**.
Standing there, I reflected on its connection to the legendary Marathon story: after the Battle of Marathon, a soldier ran 42 km to Athens to deliver news of victory,
inspiring the modern marathon race.

### Temple of Olympian Zeus & Hadrian’s Arch

Nearby stood the massive remains of the **Temple of Olympian Zeus**, which once had 104 columns, of which only 15 remain.
Just beside it, **Hadrian’s Arch** symbolically separated the “ancient city of Theseus” from “Hadrian’s new Athens.”
It was fascinating to see how layers of rulers shaped Athens’ identity.

### Acropolis

The highlight of the day was the **Acropolis**, rising above the city with monuments that define classical Greece.
We passed through the **Propylaea**, the grand gateway, and visited:

* **Temple of Athena Nike** – celebrating victory in war.
* **Parthenon** – the masterpiece dedicated to Athena, once housing a giant gold-and-ivory statue of the goddess.
* **Erechtheion** – with its famous Caryatids, the columns sculpted as female figures.

Our guide noted that the east was the entrance (where the sun rises) and the west the exit, symbolizing life and departure.
What fascinated me most were stories of its turbulent history: damaged by wars, once used as a gunpowder storage (causing catastrophic explosions),
and attacked by Venetians and Ottomans.

### Acropolis Museum

Just downhill, the **Acropolis Museum** stands atop excavated remains of ancient neighborhoods, visible through its glass floors.
Inside, artifacts from the Acropolis — including the Caryatids and sculptures — are displayed with breathtaking views of the Parthenon through floor-to-ceiling windows.
It was a reminder of how modern Athens preserves its past while embracing the present.

### Evening in Plaka & Syntagma Square

In the evening, we walked to **Plaka**, Athens’ oldest neighborhood.
Its cobblestone alleys, neoclassical houses, and lively tavernas made it a perfect spot for evening shopping.
We explored Adriano Street, Filomousou Etairias Square, and Anafiotika, tucked like a Cycladic island village beneath the Acropolis.

From there, we headed to **Syntagma Square**, where we admired the **Parliament House** and the historic **Hotel Grande Bretagne**.
A fun moment came when my son, craving McDonald’s, discovered one opposite the Parliament.
The wait time? **45 minutes** for “fast food.” The €1 soft-serve ice cream was worth it, though!
We even chatted with a young man of Indian origin who confirmed this chaos was a daily norm.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* The Parthenon has been a temple, church, mosque, and even an ammunition storage — damaged catastrophically when gunpowder ignited in the 17th century.
* The word “marathon” comes directly from the soldier who ran from the battlefield to Athens.
* Plaka’s Anafiotika neighborhood was built by craftsmen from the island of Anafi, who recreated their home’s Cycladic style under the Acropolis.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Wear comfortable shoes — the Acropolis involves climbing uneven marble steps in the heat.
2. Start early if possible; Athens can be scorching — it was **39°C** when we visited.
3. The Acropolis Museum is a must; it provides context and allows you to see artifacts up close.
4. Plaka is perfect for souvenir shopping (ceramics, olive products, jewelry) at more reasonable prices than Oia or Fira in Santorini.
5. Don’t expect “fast” service at McDonald’s near Syntagma Square — but the €1 soft-serve is a lifesaver in the heat.

## Day 7 – Meteora: Monasteries in the Sky

### Morning – Journey to Meteora

We set off at **8:00 AM** for the long drive from Athens to Meteora.
A short break at **Molos–Agios Konstantinos**, by the Maliak Gulf, gave us a chance to stretch and enjoy the view.
By noon, we reached **Kalampaka**, the town nestled at the base of Meteora’s cliffs.
Lunch at *Efrosino* was authentic and hearty — stuffed peppers, beans, rice, and sautéed vegetables reminded us why Greek food is simple yet deeply satisfying.

### Afternoon – Visiting the Monasteries

**Meteora**, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1988, is famous for its monasteries perched precariously atop sandstone pillars.
Once there were more than 40 monasteries; today, only six remain active, each with its own unique identity:

1. **Great Meteoron Monastery (Megalo Meteoro):** The oldest and largest, founded in the 14th century. Known for its scale and impressive frescoes. *(We visited.)*
2. **Varlaam Monastery:** The second largest, founded by two monk brothers. Renowned for its stunning frescoes and Byzantine artifacts. *(We visited.)*
3. **Rousanou Monastery:** Now a nunnery, cared for by a community of nuns, with beautifully decorated terraces filled with flowers. *(We visited.)*
4. **Holy Trinity Monastery (Agia Triada):** Famous for its dramatic perch and for being featured in the James Bond movie *For Your Eyes Only*. Today, it is inhabited by just one monk. *(We saw this from a distance but did not climb the 300+ steps in the extreme heat.)*
5. **St. Stephen’s Monastery (Agios Stefanos):** A women’s convent, easily accessible with a bridge instead of stairs. Known for its charitable work. *(Not visited.)*
6. **St. Nicholas Anapausas Monastery:** The smallest monastery, famous for its frescoes painted by Theophanes the Cretan in the 16th century. *(Not visited.)*

We entered three monasteries: **Great Meteoron**, **Varlaam**, and **Rousanou**.
Each offered frescoes, chapels, and history that felt alive.
Walking their grounds, it was easy to understand why monks sought these remote pillars — isolation gave both safety and spiritual clarity.

### Evening – Return to Athens

By late afternoon, the heat was at its peak — **42°C** — making the long climb up stairs and paths all the more challenging.
Still, Meteora was breathtaking, easily one of the highlights of the entire trip.
The return journey was tiring, but I left with the feeling that Meteora is a place I could return to again and again, not just for history, but for peace and reflection.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* At its peak, 40+ monasteries stood in Meteora; now only six are active.
* Holy Trinity Monastery is famous worldwide for its James Bond cameo, and today has only one monk in residence.
* Rousanou and St. Stephen’s are nunneries, offering a different kind of hospitality and care compared to the others.
* In the past, monks used ladders and rope baskets to reach the monasteries, pulling themselves and supplies up the cliffs.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Plan your visits: each monastery has its own opening hours, and not all can be entered in one day.
2. Dress codes apply: men must wear long pants, women skirts — wraps are usually provided at entrances.
3. If you have time, stay overnight in **Kalampaka** or **Kastraki** for a slower, more immersive experience.
4. Be prepared for steep climbs: some monasteries require hundreds of steps.
5. Don’t miss **Rousanou** — the flower-filled terraces make it one of the most beautiful.
6. Even if you don’t enter **Holy Trinity**, seeing it from afar is unforgettable.

## Day 8 – Mykonos: Ornos Beach, Old Town, Windmills & Little Venice

### Early Morning – Ferry Ride to Mykonos

We left Athens very early for our ferry from **Piraeus Port** to Mykonos on the **EuroChampion Jet**.
It was our first time on a high-speed jet vessel — smooth in sailing, but chaotic in baggage handling.
Passengers left their luggage piled in one large section, and retrieving it on arrival was a stressful scene with cars trying to unload at the same time.

We reached Mykonos late morning, exhausted. Our hotel, **Yiannaki Hotel**, was located near **Ornos Beach**.
After check-in, we gave in to our fatigue and crashed for a much-needed nap before venturing out.

### Afternoon – Ornos Beach

Just a **10-minute walk** from our hotel, Ornos Beach was our first stop.
It was picturesque but felt more curated and tourist-heavy than authentic.
Sunbeds lined the beach, with cafes and restaurants offering food and drinks.
While convenient, it lacked the raw beauty of some other Greek beaches we’d later see.

### Evening – Mykonos Old Town (Chora)

By late afternoon, we hopped on the local **KTEL bus** from Ornos to **Fabrika Square** in Mykonos Town.
Tickets were only **€2 each** — a bargain compared to the **€56 Uber quote** for the same short trip!
On the bus, my wife overheard another conversation: car rentals were **€700 per day**.
Clearly, Mykonos comes with a hefty price tag if you don’t use local transport.

The bus ride itself was a minor adventure: narrow, winding lanes meant our bus squeezed past a full-size coach and even a petrol tanker.
It was both nerve-wracking and impressive to see how the drivers navigated those roads.

Once in Chora, the real magic of Mykonos unfolded:

* **Panagia Paraportiani Church:** An iconic whitewashed church complex, simple yet striking.
* **Little Venice:** A waterfront neighborhood where houses with colorful balconies literally hang over the sea.
  It’s the most photographed part of Mykonos — and the most crowded. Watching the sunset from here was beautiful, but the hype meant jostling for space.
* **Mykonos Windmills:** Perched on a hill, these historic windmills once milled grain but now serve as postcard-perfect backdrops.
  Sadly, they seemed poorly maintained, but their silhouette against the sunset was still unforgettable.
* **Matoyianni Street:** A lively shopping street filled with boutiques.
  While charming, the prices were staggering — simple cotton shirts starting at €50 and luxury labels like Gucci and Prada dominating the strip.

We strolled until late evening, enjoying gelato, taking photos, and soaking in the atmosphere before catching the bus back.

### Late Night – Safe but Costly

We returned around 10:30 PM. One thing that stood out: despite the tourist crowds, **Mykonos felt very safe**,
even for solo female travelers late at night.
However, we also realized that most shops and eateries here preferred **cash over credit cards**, something to keep in mind.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* Mykonos Town’s narrow alleys were deliberately designed to confuse pirates during the island’s turbulent past.
* The windmills, now purely decorative, once symbolized Mykonos’ importance as a grain milling hub.
* Despite being a jet-set hotspot, local buses are still the most practical (and adventurous) way to get around.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Take the bus, not Uber or taxis — it’s safe, cheap, and part of the experience.
2. Ornos Beach is convenient but feels very commercial. For a quieter experience, explore other beaches if you have time.
3. Don’t miss Little Venice at sunset, but expect heavy crowds and queues for the perfect photo spot.
4. Shopping in Mykonos is pricey — souvenirs are better bought in Athens or Santorini’s Fira.
5. Despite its commercialism, Mykonos is worth it for its evening energy and iconic photo spots.

---

## Day 9 – Santorini: Arrival & Oia Sunset

### Afternoon – Ferry from Mykonos to Santorini

We left Mykonos on the **EuroChampion Jet** bound for Santorini.
Like our earlier ferry, the baggage system was chaotic, with passengers scrambling at the end to retrieve bags while vehicles tried to unload at the same time.
The ferry was delayed by about **45 minutes**, arriving in Santorini around **3:30 PM** instead of 2:45.

Unlike other transfers, we didn’t see a driver holding our name placard at the port.
After some anxious calls to the manager, a taxi finally came — but it was noticeably not the sleek Mercedes we had gotten used to in Turkey.
The driver’s demeanor was curt, reflecting our first brush with Santorini’s more businesslike side of tourism.

### First Impressions of Santorini

The island immediately struck me with its rugged cliffs and steep terrain — a sharp contrast to Mykonos’ flatter, whitewashed landscape.
Our hotel, **Aegean Plaza**, was located in **Kamari**, near the famous black-sand beach.
By the time we reached, it was already 4:30 PM, leaving just enough time to freshen up before chasing the island’s most famous view: the sunset in Oia.

### Evening – Journey to Oia

From Kamari, we relied on Santorini’s **KTEL bus service** — clean, efficient, and reliable.
It required two legs:

* Kamari → Fira (~25 minutes)
* Fira → Oia (~35 minutes)

We reached Oia just before **7:00 PM**. The weather was hot (**29°C but felt like 32°C**), and after a long day of travel, we were already exhausted.
Still, nothing could stop us from exploring this postcard-perfect town.

### Exploring Oia

Oia is stunning, but also overwhelmingly **commercialized**.
Nearly every whitewashed building with its blue dome had been converted into a boutique hotel or restaurant.
This meant that finding a good photo spot often meant bumping into queues of tourists waiting their turn.

We walked up and down the marble streets, stopping at various vantage points for pictures.
The **blue-domed churches** against the backdrop of the caldera were breathtaking, though getting that “perfect shot” often meant waiting in line.
I realized that the famous “sunset with dome” view is harder to capture than Instagram suggests.

Finally, as the sun dipped into the Aegean, we joined the crowds at Oia’s western edge.
The golden glow across the town and caldera was indeed spectacular, even if the experience felt less intimate due to the sheer number of people.

### Dinner Back in Kamari

We returned late, around **10:30 PM**, and grabbed dinner at a small gyro shop near the bus stop in Kamari.
The food was dry and not to our taste, but it did the job.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* Oia’s architecture — whitewashed walls and blue domes — isn’t just aesthetic; the white reflects the sun, while domes help circulate air in churches.
* Santorini’s bus system (**KTEL**) is one of the most reliable and safest ways to get around the island.
* Locals warn not to drink or even brush with tap water due to its high sulfur content.

---

### Key Takeaways & Recommendations

1. Expect crowds in Oia — if you want prime sunset spots, arrive at least 1–2 hours early.
2. Use buses instead of taxis — they’re cheap, reliable, and avoid the island’s crazy taxi prices.
3. Don’t expect privacy in Oia hotels — rooms often open directly onto busy paths.
4. Kamari is a good base for families — quieter, with easy bus access to Fira and Oia.
5. Manage expectations: the sunset is magical, but the experience is shared with thousands of others.

---

## Day 10 – Santorini: Fira Morning & Catamaran Cruise

### Morning – Exploring Fira

We started early, taking the bus from **Kamari** to **Fira**, the island’s capital.
Compared to Oia the previous night, Fira felt calmer in the morning — cooler, less crowded, and easier to explore.

Walking along the cliffside paths, we enjoyed sweeping views of the **caldera**,
the volcanic sea basin that defines Santorini’s geography.
Fira’s narrow streets were lined with shops selling jewelry, ceramics, olive products, and souvenirs.
Prices weren’t dramatically cheaper than Oia, but we still ended up doing quite a bit of shopping —
though in the process, I lost my selfie stick in one of the stores.

One jewelry shop stood out with **unique designs**,
so much so that I encouraged my wife to make a few special purchases — a worthwhile splurge this time,
unlike some of my impulsive buys earlier in the trip.

### Afternoon – Catamaran Cruise

At around **2:15 PM**, our driver picked us up for the highlight of the day:
a **semi-private catamaran cruise**.
The yacht carried about 15 people, including a warm and lively Gujarati family from Toronto.

We set sail across the **Aegean Sea**, passing volcanic cliffs and stopping at key spots:

* **Hot Springs:** The waters here aren’t truly hot springs but are warmed by volcanic activity.
  The high sulfur content gives the water a reddish tint. Swimming here was rough due to choppy waves.
* **Red Beach:** With its striking red volcanic cliffs, this was the best swimming stop of the trip.
  Snorkeling revealed small fish beneath the waves, and the water was refreshing.
* **White Beach:** Another stop, though this time we chose to sit out and simply enjoy the view from the deck.

Onboard, we were served a **BBQ meal** of grilled meats, seafood, and salads, with unlimited wine and drinks.
The crew kept the atmosphere lively, balancing safety with fun.

As we sailed back, we watched the **sunset from the water** — a calmer and more intimate experience compared to the crowded Oia sunset.
Despite the steep price (**€125 per person**, though others said they paid €100),
it felt like a once-in-a-lifetime splurge.

One hiccup: my son wasn’t feeling well early on, tired and restless.
Fortunately, after a short nap on deck, he perked up and enjoyed the rest of the journey.

---

## Day 11 – Kamari Beach & Farewell to Greece

### Morning – Kamari Beach

On our last day, we finally had time to enjoy **Kamari Beach**, located just a minute’s walk from our hotel in Kamari.
Unlike the sandy beaches of Mykonos, Kamari is a **black-pebble beach**, formed from volcanic activity thousands of years ago.
It’s popular for water sports, sunbeds, and beachfront cafes.

We took a short stroll along the shoreline, soaking in the morning sun.
While the beach itself was beautiful, the pebbly surface made it less ideal for long barefoot walks.
Still, it was the perfect way to mark the final morning of our vacation.

After the beach, we returned to the hotel for a hearty breakfast — the first truly relaxed breakfast of the trip,
after days of early starts and rushed meals.

### Afternoon – Departure from Santorini

Our transfer was scheduled for **2:45 PM** for the **5:15 PM flight**.
At first, I worried the timing was too tight, but **Santorini’s small airport** surprised me with its efficiency.
From curbside to gate, the process took only about **30 minutes**, including baggage and security.

While waiting, we made some last-minute purchases — **baklava** and a bottle of local **Ouzo**,
the anise-flavored spirit that Greece is famous for.
The airport’s small size actually worked in our favor, allowing us to slow down and reflect before boarding.

### Final Impressions

As the plane took off, I looked back at the caldera cliffs fading into the horizon.
This trip had been fast-paced, at times chaotic, but filled with unforgettable moments —
from the surreal landscapes of Cappadocia to the grand ruins of Ephesus, the heights of Meteora,
the sunsets of Oia, and the calm waters of the Aegean Sea.

---

### Interesting Fact/Experience

* Kamari Beach’s black pebbles are remnants of Santorini’s volcanic eruption in the 16th century BC,
  one of the most powerful eruptions in recorded history.
* Santorini Airport is tiny, but incredibly quick and efficient compared to larger European hubs.
