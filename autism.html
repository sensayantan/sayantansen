Asking research questions about autism

A search over roughly 1,900 real research papers and clinical trial records, built to answer the questions families actually ask — and to show its working, so every answer can be traced back to the paper it came from.

Why this exists

Autism diagnoses have risen sharply over the past two decades. Part of that is a genuine increase; a large part is broader diagnostic criteria and better recognition of children who would once have been missed entirely. The distinction matters for researchers. For a family, the practical consequence is the same either way: far more people are arriving at the beginning of this, holding the same handful of questions, with very little that answers them well.

Those questions are rarely exotic. Is it genetic? Did something I do cause this? What does the evidence actually say about early intervention? What is being trialled right now, and where? Honest answers to all of them exist in published research — which is precisely where most parents give up. It is written in unfamiliar vocabulary, scattered across tens of thousands of papers, and the readable summaries in between are too often produced by someone with something to sell.

This page is a small attempt at narrowing that gap: pointing a family at the actual literature on the question they asked, in the words the researchers used, with a link to every source. It answers nothing on its own authority.

Where the information comes from

Two public sources. No scraping, no paywalled content, nothing private, and no paid API.

SourceWhat it holdsHow it is accessed
PubMedUS National Library of Medicine ~1,500 abstracts of peer-reviewed autism research The free E-utilities HTTP API, in two calls: esearch returns the record IDs matching a search term, efetch returns those records as XML. No key, no account.
ClinicalTrials.govUS National Institutes of Health ~400 registered trials — what is being tried, by whom, and where Its public v2 JSON API, paged through with a cursor. No key, no account.

Every record keeps its source URL, publication date, authors and journal, so each result links back to the paper or trial it came from. Nothing on this page is written from memory. The set is refreshed automatically each month, so it does not quietly go stale.

How it works

Keyword search fails people here, because a worried parent and a journal abstract do not use the same words. "Will my son ever talk?" shares almost no vocabulary with a paper titled "Predictors of expressive language outcomes in minimally verbal children."

So each record is converted into a list of 384 numbers — an embedding — that represents what the text means rather than which words it contains. A question is converted the same way, and the records whose numbers sit closest to it are returned, ranked by how close. That similarity score is shown on every result, because a number you can see is more honest than a confident answer you cannot check.

This is the retrieval half of what is usually called RAG. It is a small demonstration of something with much wider application: the same technique turns any pile of documents nobody has time to read — clinical guidance, policy, benefits paperwork, school reports — into something a person can ask a plain question of. Autism research is a case where the gap between what is known and what reaches a family is wide, and the cost of that gap is borne by people already having a hard week.

1. Prepared questions

Eleven questions run in advance, so this works with no server at all — the results are computed offline and shipped with the page. Each result is a stored record, returned word for word and ranked. Nothing here is written or summarised by an AI.

2. Ask your own question

Anything you type has to be converted to numbers at that moment, which needs a live server — so this half runs on a Cloudflare Worker rather than on the static site. It ranks the same 1,900 records, then has a language model write a short summary of the top matches, with citations you can click through to the papers themselves.

Not connected yet. The search service for this section has not been wired up, so free-text questions are unavailable. The prepared questions above are unaffected — they need no server.

Where this goes next

The corpus is deliberately narrow right now — research abstracts and trials, nothing else. The obvious additions are prevalence and demographic figures from the CDC's monitoring network, each carrying its own citation, and the trial locations already sitting unused in the data, which could become a map of who is doing this work and where.

The larger ambition is less about search and more about the thing behind it: the distance between a family with a question and the people, services and evidence that could answer it. That distance is not a technology problem, but it is one of the places where this technology is unusually well suited to helping — and it costs nothing to run.
