Is Test Cricket now more unpredictable and can horizontal bat be a new success mantra of Test cricket batting?

By Sayantan Sen, September 6, 2014.

Post the recently concluded test series between India-England (Pataudi Trophy, Jul-Aug 2014) Sayantan Sen takes a look at an changing conditions and challenge of Test cricket and what might be the new success mantra of Test cricket batting.

Test cricket is definitely the epitome of cricketing skill. This is the format where ones skill gets tested (batsman/bowler/keeper or a fielder) and so the mental toughness and this why all cricketers dream is to play Test cricket rather be just successful in one-days or T20. But has Test cricket become more tough and unpredictable.

Columnist tries to analyze how Test cricket in 70's and 80's is so different when compared to Test cricket played in this new century. Let's Test our memory and go back to the days of 70's and 80's and how different they were....
70's and 80's Era Y2K Era
Bowling Express fast bowlers (Leagues of Juel Garners, Dennis Lillie, Marshall and Michael Holding) with one single mission to (literally) terrify the batsman with their swing, pace and bounce (bodyline). Delight to watch how batsmen would only focus on playing late, keep the bat as close as possible to the body, be aware of the forth stump and play more with their pads and "well-left" till the ball gets worn out. A mental game along with skill and techniques. Then there used to be the set of classical spinners whose focus was on spin and variations, primarily in flight and trajectory. No doosras, (It seems there is a teesra too) just traditional spin bowling. A battle between two sharp minds who is trying to over-smart each other. While Fast bowling has become more taxing for the individuals, bowlers have become really smart and typically uses tricks that were never known earlier. Showers is their biggest weapon along with Reverse Swing. Actually now fast bowlers wait to get the ball old and sessions post Tea is most exciting in Test cricket now a days due to Reverse Swing.  Fast bowlers now believe in teasing and tempting the batsman more with their nagging line and length rather than their body-line techniques. Hence we see Anderson, McGrath and Vernon Philander to be more successful. May be Brett Lee and Shoib Akhtar would have been more talked about fast bowlers if they were born may be 20 years back :-). Spin bowling has also revolutionized over past two decades. It seems spins bowlers who can bowl straight are more lethal. Although we were gifted with un-arguably  2 best spinners (Warne and Murali) in 90s, but world-wide now the spinners who are more successful are the one who can bowl doosras (Ajmal) and can drift the ball (is it swing bowling?? ) more effectively in the air (Grahme Swann)
Fielding During these time, few set of field positions used to play a very crutial role,  and hence got legends created. History would always remember Gavaskar and Mark Waugh in the slips, Kirmani & Ian Healey for their keeping skills against the spinners and Eknath Solkar at forward short leg position. For pacers slip cordon was the classical trap along with third man & deep square-leg for their body-line tactics Captains who can put fielders in un-orthodox positions are considered more innovative & thinking captain. Shane Warne showcased great cricketing mind putting un-orthodox gully and leg-slip during the inaugural IPL T20 tournament and made everyone eventually agree to one of the most arguable statement "Best Captain not to lead Team Australia ever".  In Test cricket now, putting two third mans is considered more flamboyant that to keep more than two or three slips.
Pitch, Weather & Tours Even the pitch and weather condition seems to be very predictable across the world during that time. You would always get swinging and overcast condition in Lords, flat pitch with cracks in Eden Gardens and  Bounce like a tennis ball at Perth. You always had cricket being played only during Christmas in Down-Under and during British summer in England. There also used to be a known set of tours for every cricketing nations, hence their preparation was more focused. You tend to hear stories how Grahame Gooch mastered sweep just to tackle India Spin wizards and Mohinder Amarnath build his hooking skill over the years to tackle the express pace of West-Indians. Now-a-days pitch curators love to bring in variations in pitch conditions and it is more of a gamble for the captain to choose between batting and bowling and hence prefer more to lose tosses. Now we tend to get more sedate and playable pitch in Perth and rather swinging one in Mohali and a bowlers night-mare (as flat as possible) in Lords. Not sure if these variations are by chance or by design, but definitely brings a lot of uncertainty. As far as tour goes, as mentioned earlier, it will not be far off when to accommodate players calendar of playing T20 tournaments back-to-back, Test-Series between two nations might happen once in four/five years.
Cricket Ball & Bat manufactures There were always predominantly 3 to 4 varieties. Always weighed between 5 1/2 and 5 3/4 ounces, dyed only in red. In Australia, you play with Kookaburra balls, In England and West Indies, you play with Dukes while in sub-continents you play with SG. Even there were very limited number of bat manufacturers across the world, and batsmen never used to believe in experimenting too much with their bat weight or on the number of batting grips on their handle. Yes there were players who used heavy bats (Clive Llyod dragging his bat along), but there was also a time in Indian cricket team when almost 10 out of 11 players were using Sunny-Tonny bat (Gavaskar made it famous). Although cricket ball variations has been limited (especially after 1990 ICC Cricket world cup when white "duke" ball was used in place of traditional Kookabura ball), Bats on the contrary has gone through revolutions. Bat manufacturers are now many and using bottom heavy bat is now a norm and the way the ball flies outside the cricketing park, one would wonder whether it is do with players wrist strength, or timing or just wood-works. In this century different manufacturers have tried the bats to be more powerful within the norms of the ICC Rules and hence there has been   introduction of technologies like " Carbon fiber-reinforced polymer support down the spine of the bat used by Ricky Ponting during 2005" and "usage of carbon fibre handle, the C6 and C6+, which weighed 3 ounces/85 grams less than a standard laminated cane and rubber handle.
All-rounder All-rounder's were always respected and valued, but Test cricket during this era always depended on specialist. Apart from few exceptions of Gary Sobers (cricketer of that caliber will be immortal and always be the top buy in any auction), Iam Botham, Kapil Dev or Imran Khan at-least ten out of eleven players in the team was either a specialist batsman, or a special bowler or a specials keeper.  Hence players like Muddasar Nassar or Derek Pringle were regulars in their One-Day squad, but never in their Test team. This generation of test cricket and the coming generation might see 80% of the team will have all-rounders. Moreover cricket playing nations now build their Test squad after looking at the performance in One-dayers or T20 where being all-rounder is a necessity not a lukury. While Jacque Kallis might be the arguably the best all-rounder of all time playing in this era, but one should not get surprised to see players like Ravindra Jadeja playing similar role for India Test Team, just because he can bat an bit, field a bit, bowl a bit and on his day can beat a team single-handedly.
Money & Entertainment Never ever the cricketing gurus during that time were non-cricketers. All cricket journalists had knowledge as much as any cricketer and only talked about cricket and nothing else.  Moreover Test Cricket was gentlemen's game where sledging was a trick only practiced by the Aussies. Business and Money were enablers but not drivers. Movie Stars were only seen in Sharjah. In this modern era, beyond Test Cricket, other formats are more exciting and has 3E (Entertainment-Entertainment-Entertainment) tag associated with them. Remember Mandira Bedi coming along with Charu Sharma during 2003 ICC World Cup?? . Test cricket is squeezed in-between big ICC tournaments and T20 Criketainment extravagance across all Cricket playing nations (Almost all cricket-playing nation  has their own T20-20 tournament). Test cricket has now more vacant pavilions than ever before.

This columnist wants to highlight that for Test cricket in the current era, almost everything has changed and definitely this has heavily impacted the glam component of any cricket team, the top order batsmen. True legends like Gavaskar, Viswanath, Zaheer Abbas, Gordon Greenidge of 70s and 80s and as late as Sachin & Dravid would be first remembered as a Test Cricket Batsman. These guys were epitome of classic cricket batting skill. But so we see such skills now in abundance in Test cricket? Is the definition of copy-book batting techniques changing? In this modern era, being technically correct is sufficient to be a successful Test batsman?

Test cricket has definitely become more challenging and result oriented (Test-cricket getting over in 3 days is almost a new norm now) and the biggest impact that has created is on the batting score card. Now score-card like 8-4 (8 runs for 4 wicket, what we saw in India-Eng 4th Test match, at Old Trafford, Manchester, 7-Aug-14) is not a rare phenomenon. General word of wisdom says that play your natural game. What is batsmen's natural game now-a-days. Leave the ball or slash hard at the ball? Tuck the bouncers or play horizontal bat shot? It seems natural instinct is now to do later.  Is there anything wrong there? Spectators would like to rather see a Peterson or Segwag type century rather a painfully long and nagging 2 day elapsed century innings.

Playing natural is lot positive and easy for any batsman, than playing straight and defensive, just for the fact that one is playing Test cricket. If we look back at the just concluded India-England Test series, M.S Dhoni was India's most successful batsman. Columnist don't feel that Dhoni tried play differently? Most of Dhoni's innings were full of horizontal bat shots, but was more patient and was more gritty in his approach (I will not throw my wicket attitude). Actually implying the simple rule of bat position, when the ball gets the edge of a straight bat, the probability of ball going to slip cordon's comfort zone is much more, when compared to a ball getting the edge of a horizontal bat. AB de Villiers is another classic example. The angle at which his bat hits the ball, it has always an element of Golf and Baseball type swing, which actually improves the chance of better timing and powerful edges (if ball doesn't hit the middle of bat). Kohli's out of form (in the recently concluded Test series) might have some link where he may have been trying to connect the ball much straighter with his bat. Kohli just like AB, has a very unique bat angle which gives him that immense power and timing. May be India's top order batsmen in the recently concluded Test series should have played their natural game coupled with more concentration and may be less risk &  flamboyance. Columnist feels that Australia's  revival in last 1 year has been predominantly due to being able to successfully rely on natural stroke-players whose mental strength was at its peak. David Warner, previously considered only in shorter form is now Aussies natural choice as a Test opener. Steven Smith who can never be considered as orthodox Test batsmen is now a regular in their Test team.

Test cricket hence needs batsmen who plays natural (horizontal or straight) and rather doesn't change their playing approach, just because they are playing Test cricket. What definitely is needed though is mental strength and the urge to stay at the crease. During 70s and 80s, every cricket playing nations top 3 batsman were copy-book straight bat players. Gavaskar, Grahamme Gooch, Greenidge,  Aravinda De Silva, Geoff March... No exception at all. In the current era, just look who are successful in these positions now. Shewag, David Warner, AB de Villiers, Chris Gale... They are all powerful natural stroke player, who has the mental strength, guts & agility to overcome changing conditions of Test cricket.

While we know that there is no question that natural copy-book batsmen will be succesfull in Test (Dravid, Chanderpaul, Hashim Amla, Jayawardane etc are all in that league), but this is the time to think and accept that even natural stroke makers whose style is not always copy-book  can be equally or even more successful. Columnist feels India Team lost the opportunity to get the service of Yuvraj Singh in Test cricket and ended up making him a specialist one-day player. Can we afford to do the same now? Can't Suresh Raina (for India) or David Miller (for South Africa) be part of their Test Team, before their mental condition dips down from the current peak and they are no longer are suitable to play this format of the game?
